01 - Behavioral Fundamentals

1. What interviewers actually assess

Behavioral rounds predict how you will work, not whether you are likeable. Typical signals (names vary by company):

  • Ownership: did you drive things to done, beyond your ticket?
  • Collaboration and conflict: can you disagree without damage and still ship?
  • Ambiguity: can you make progress without perfect info?
  • Learning and feedback: do you change after failure or critique?
  • Impact: did your work matter, and can you show it with evidence?
  • Judgment and communication: clear, honest, structured, self-aware.
  • Leadership (at any level): influencing without authority, mentoring, unblocking others.

Interviewers usually write notes against a rubric and compare candidates. Give them concrete, quotable evidence. Vague answers produce "no signal", which counts against you.

2. Methods: STAR and CAR

STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. CAR: Context, Action, Result (shorter; good for quick follow-ups).

Time budget for a 2-3 minute answer: | Part | Share | Notes | |---|---|---| | Situation/Task | 15% | One or two sentences: stakes, your role | | Action | 55% | The bulk. Specific decisions, what YOU did, why | | Result | 20% | Numbers, outcome, what changed | | Learning | 10% | What you do differently now |

Common mistakes: long setup; "we" everywhere; no result; result with no number; no reflection; blaming others.

3. Pick and structure 8-10 reusable stories

Build a bank once; map each story to several questions.

Selection criteria: recent (last 2-3 years where possible), you had a real role, there was a challenge, and a measurable or observable outcome. Include at least 2 failures or mistakes, 1 conflict, 1 ambiguity, 1 leadership/mentoring, 1 tight deadline, 1 receiving hard feedback, 1 customer/user-facing, 1 technical deep dive.

No job experience? Use: capstone/course projects, hackathons, open-source PRs, tutoring, volunteering, a job in another field (retail, teaching, ops all have conflict, deadlines, customers).

Structure per story: one-line headline, STAR bullets, 2-3 numbers, the "I" actions, the learning, and 3 anticipated follow-ups.

5. Questions by theme, model outlines, weak vs strong

Conflict

Questions: "Tell me about a disagreement with a teammate." "Someone pushed back on your design." Model outline: context and the genuine disagreement (technical, not personal) -> you sought to understand their view (asked questions, restated) -> found shared goal and data (benchmark, prototype, user evidence) -> decision method (experiment, tech lead call, compromise) -> outcome and relationship afterwards -> learning.

  • Weak: "He was wrong, I proved it." Or "I avoided conflict." No resolution. Villainizing.
  • Strong: you can state the other side's best argument; you used evidence; you committed to the decision even if you lost ("disagree and commit"); the relationship improved.

Failure / mistake

Questions: "Biggest failure?" "A time you shipped a bug." Outline: real mistake with real consequences -> own it plainly (no "my weakness is perfectionism") -> immediate mitigation -> root cause -> systemic fix (test, alert, checklist, review step) -> evidence it didn't recur.

  • Weak: fake failure, blaming others, no lesson, or a catastrophic one with no recovery.
  • Strong: specific, owned, calm, process improvement that outlasted you.

Leadership

Questions: "Led without authority." "Mentored someone." "Took initiative." Outline: gap you noticed -> how you got buy-in (small pilot, doc, 1:1s) -> how you split work/enabled others -> outcome for the team, not only you.

  • Weak: "I told people what to do"; hero story where you did everything.
  • Strong: you multiplied others, handled pushback, shared credit, measured the outcome.

Ambiguity

Questions: "Unclear requirements." "Started a project with little direction." Outline: what was unknown -> how you reduced it (questions to stakeholders, assumptions listed, small experiment/prototype, narrow v1) -> checkpoints for feedback -> decision with incomplete info -> outcome.

  • Weak: waited for instructions or built the wrong thing in silence.
  • Strong: wrote down assumptions, validated cheaply, communicated early, adapted.

Deadlines / pressure

Questions: "Tight deadline." "Too much to do." Outline: constraint and stakes -> prioritized by impact/risk -> cut scope explicitly with stakeholder agreement -> communicated risks early -> delivered, with quality guardrails -> aftermath (paid down shortcuts).

  • Weak: heroics/all-nighters as the only lever; cutting tests silently.
  • Strong: negotiated scope, surfaced risk early, protected what mattered.

Feedback

Questions: "Hard feedback you received." "Feedback you gave." Outline: the feedback (specific) -> your initial reaction honestly -> how you clarified and acted -> evidence of change -> later, how you now seek feedback. For giving: private, specific, kind, behavior-based, follow-up.

  • Weak: defensive, trivial feedback, "I can't think of any."
  • Strong: concrete behavior change with a visible result.

Impact

Questions: "Proudest accomplishment." "Biggest impact." Outline: problem and who cared -> why you picked it -> approach and trade-offs -> metric movement -> second-order effects (others reused it).

  • Weak: describes activity, not outcome; no numbers.
  • Strong: before/after numbers, clear link between your actions and the result.

Ownership

Questions: "Went beyond your role." "Took responsibility for something that wasn't yours." Outline: noticed a problem nobody owned -> took it, with agreement -> drove through obstacles -> handed off or maintained -> result.

  • Weak: "That's not my job" tone, or overstepping without telling anyone.
  • Strong: communicated, finished, made it sustainable (docs, tests, owner).

Also prepare

"Why this company/role?", "Why are you changing careers?" (frame as a deliberate path with evidence: projects, learning), "Tell me about yourself" (present -> past -> future, 90 seconds), "What are you looking for?", "Weakness" (real, with mitigation).

6. Company-values themes (generic; verify against the company's current published values)

Companies publish culture/leadership principles and often map questions to them. Do not assume specifics; read the careers site and any official interview-prep page the recruiter sends.

  • Google-style "Googleyness" themes (generic): comfort with ambiguity, collaboration and humility, bias to action, doing the right thing, intellectual curiosity, caring about users, handling conflict constructively, and for some levels "leadership" and "role-related knowledge". Prepare stories for each. Verify against Google's current official candidate guidance; names and weighting change.
  • Uber-style themes (generic): ownership and accountability, moving fast under real-world operational complexity (marketplaces, reliability, scale), customer/rider/driver obsession, data-driven decisions, resilience, and respectful debate then commitment. Verify against Uber's current published values and recruiter guidance.

Method: for each published value, tag 1-2 stories from your bank. If a value has no story, write one.

7. Delivery checklist

  • Speak 2-3 minutes; stop and ask "Want more detail on X?"
  • Use "I" for actions; credit the team once.
  • Include one number and one learning.
  • Avoid confidential details of past employers; anonymize names and figures where needed.
  • Practice out loud; record; cut filler. Do not memorize a script word for word; memorize the bullets.
